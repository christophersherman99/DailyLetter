The DCF Lab

Build a discounted cash flow valuation yourself, one step at a time, from a company's real financial statements. Every number is yours to change; the explanations show what each one means and where to find it in a 10-K.

Step 1

Know the business

In the 10-K: Item 8, Financial Statements. Revenue and operating income are on the income statement; depreciation, capital expenditures and working capital are on the cash flow statement.

Step 2

Forecast revenue

A DCF values a company by the cash it will produce in the future, so it starts with how fast sales will grow. Most models forecast five years in detail, then assume a slower, steady rate forever.

Anchor on evidence: the company's own track record, and what analysts expect for the next two years. Growth usually fades toward the economy's long-run pace (2 to 3%) as a company matures.

Step 3

From revenue to free cash flow

Investors are paid from free cash flow: the cash left after running the business and reinvesting in it. For a DCF of the whole company we use free cash flow to the firm, before any payments to lenders or shareholders:

FCFF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + D&A − capital spending − increase in working capital

  • EBIT (operating profit) is what the business earns before interest and taxes.
  • D&A (depreciation and amortization) is subtracted in EBIT but costs no cash this year, so it is added back.
  • Capital spending (capex) is cash invested in new restaurants, stores or equipment.
  • Working capital is cash tied up in inventory and receivables; growing usually needs more.

Each is set as a percentage of revenue, using the history in Step 1 as a guide.

Step 4

The discount rate (WACC)

A dollar in five years is worth less than a dollar today, and a risky dollar is worth less still. The discount rate is the return investors demand. Because the business is financed by both shareholders and lenders, we blend their required returns by how much of each the company uses: the weighted average cost of capital.

Cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium  (CAPM)

WACC = equity weight × cost of equity + debt weight × cost of debt × (1 − tax rate)

Beta measures how much the stock moves with the market; the equity risk premium is the extra return investors expect from stocks over Treasuries, commonly 4 to 6%. Interest is tax deductible, which is why debt is cheaper after tax.

Step 5

Terminal value

The company keeps going after year five, so we capture everything beyond it in one number, the terminal value. Two standard ways, which make a good cross-check on each other:

  • Perpetuity growth (Gordon): year-6 cash flow ÷ (WACC − growth). The growth rate must stay below the economy's long-run growth, or the company eventually becomes the economy.
  • Exit multiple: what the business might sell for, as a multiple of EBITDA, based on what similar companies trade at today.

The terminal value is often more than half of the total. That is why small changes here matter so much.

Step 6

From enterprise value to a share price

Discounting every cash flow and the terminal value to today gives enterprise value: the value of the whole business. Lenders are paid first, so subtract debt and add back cash to get the equity value, then divide by shares outstanding.
